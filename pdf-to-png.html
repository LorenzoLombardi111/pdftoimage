When is PNG better than JPG for PDF conversion?

JPG compression works by discarding information that human eyes are less sensitive to. This works beautifully for photographs but poorly for text and line art. When you convert a text-heavy PDF page to JPG, you'll notice subtle halos around letterforms, slightly fuzzy lines in diagrams, and color banding in gradients — especially at lower quality settings. PNG eliminates all of these issues. Every character is pixel-perfect, every line is crisp, and every color is accurate. If your PDF contains code listings, financial tables, architectural drawings, circuit diagrams, flowcharts, or any content where precision matters, PNG is the unambiguous choice. freepdftoimage.app makes switching to PNG instant — just click the PNG toggle in the settings panel.

How do PNG file sizes compare to JPG?

PNG files are larger than equivalent JPGs because lossless compression retains all image data. A typical letter-size PDF page converted at 2x resolution might produce a 150 KB JPG at 85% quality, compared to a 400-800 KB PNG. At 4x resolution, PNGs can reach 1-3 MB per page. This is the fundamental tradeoff: perfect quality costs more storage. For most workflows — saving to local disk, inserting into presentations, archiving documents — this size difference is negligible on modern hardware. Where size matters (email attachments, web publishing, mobile-constrained environments), consider whether the lossless fidelity is actually needed. If you're converting photo-heavy PDF pages where minor compression is acceptable, JPG may serve you better. For everything else, PNG's clarity justifies the extra kilobytes.

What resolution should I use for PNG?

Since PNG is lossless, resolution is the primary factor controlling both quality and file size. The 2x default (144 DPI) works well for screen display — presentations, web pages, and digital documents look sharp without excessive file sizes. For technical documentation where small text must remain readable when zoomed, 3x (216 DPI) provides extra headroom. For print output, 4x (288 DPI) approaches the 300 DPI standard that most printers expect. At 1x (72 DPI), PNG files are smallest, but text may look slightly soft on high-DPI displays. Your only variable is resolution, which makes the decision simpler.
